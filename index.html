01 / A FAMILIAR SCENE

The assignment you didn't really write.

It's 11 PM. You have an assignment due tomorrow.

You open ChatGPT just to get some ideas. It gives you an outline. You ask it to improve your argument. Then you ask it to write an introduction.

Within minutes, you have almost everything you need.

You finish faster than expected. But the next morning, your professor asks: “Can you explain why you made this argument?”

Would you be able to explain it without opening ChatGPT again?

This is the question behind a growing conversation about generative AI. We're getting better at completing tasks with AI. But are we always getting better at understanding what we're doing?

03 / THE HIDDEN TRADE-OFF

What are we actually outsourcing?

Think about a calculator. You enter a problem and get an answer without doing every step in your head. AI can do something similar—but with much more complicated tasks.

It can create ideas, organize arguments, explain concepts, compare information, and even recommend decisions.

Psychologists call this cognitive offloading: using a tool to reduce the mental work we would otherwise do ourselves.

Cognitive offloading isn't automatically bad. It can free us to focus on other important things. The question is which parts of thinking we're handing over.

04 / LOOK AT THE RESEARCH

Faster answers.
Less effort?

In a 2025 study, Lee and colleagues surveyed 319 knowledge workers and collected 936 examples of people using generative AI on work tasks.

319knowledge workers surveyed
936examples of AI-assisted tasks

They found that greater confidence in AI was associated with less reported critical-thinking engagement. Higher confidence in one's own ability was associated with more.

What the study found QUALITATIVE SUMMARY

Higher confidence in AI
Less critical-thinking engagement
Higher confidence in yourself
More critical-thinking engagement

These arrows illustrate reported associations—not measured percentages, causal effects, or changes in intelligence. Source: Lee et al. (2025).

The study doesn't prove that AI makes people less intelligent. It suggests that how much we trust AI can influence how we approach thinking.

05 / TWO DIFFERENT APPROACHES

Finishing isn't the same as understanding.

Imagine two students studying for an exam. They both finish preparing, but their processes look different.

STUDENT A

Thinks first

  1. Reads the chapter
  2. Writes their own notes
  3. Tries practice problems
  4. Asks AI to explain mistakes
STUDENT B

Asks first

  1. Asks AI for a summary
  2. Reads the summary
  3. Uses AI-generated answers
  4. Reviews before the exam

Does Student B definitely perform worse? No. We'd need actual learning assessments to know. But the example raises an important question:

Are we using AI to help ourselves learn—or mainly to avoid the hard parts of learning?

06 / SPOT THE PROBLEM INTERACTIVE

Would you catch the mistake?

Two explanations answer the same question: Why do astronauts appear weightless while orbiting Earth?

ANSWER A

Astronauts float because they and their spacecraft are falling around Earth together. Gravity still acts on them, but they experience apparent weightlessness.

ANSWER B

Astronauts float because Earth's gravity becomes almost zero at the altitude of the International Space Station. With hardly any gravity acting on them, they become weightless.

Which answer is more accurate?

The point isn't astronomy. It's about judging information that sounds convincing. When we don't already know a subject well, confident-sounding mistakes can be hard to catch.

07 / ANOTHER IMPORTANT SKILL

Do you understand it—or does it just sound familiar?

Have you ever read an AI explanation and thought, “That makes perfect sense”—until someone asks you to explain it in your own words?

This connects to metacognition: being aware of your own thinking and understanding.

Do I actually understand this?
How confident should I be?
What evidence supports this?
What information am I missing?

Tankelevitch and colleagues (2024) argue that generative AI creates new demands on our ability to monitor our thinking, evaluate AI responses, and decide when to rely on them.

Put simply: AI makes answers easy to get. Deciding whether they deserve our trust is still our responsibility.

09 / A BETTER APPROACH

You don't have to stop using AI.

AI has real benefits. It can make complex information easier to access, provide feedback, and save time. The goal isn't to reject it. It's to stay involved in the thinking.

01

Try first. Prompt second.

Spend a few minutes forming your own idea before asking AI.

02

Ask for feedback, not just answers.

Try: “What are the weaknesses in my argument?”

03

Check the original source.

Especially for facts, quotes, statistics, and research claims.

04

Explain it without AI.

Can you explain the idea later, without reopening the chat?

05

Challenge the response.

Ask what assumptions it makes and what evidence might contradict it.

10 / ONE LAST QUESTION

Next time you open AI, who's doing the thinking?

AI DOES THE THINKING

Ask → Get answer → Submit

AI SUPPORTS THINKING

Think → Ask → Evaluate → Decide

“Am I using AI because it helps me understand—or because I don't want to think through it myself?”

AI can help us do more. The challenge is making sure it doesn't make thinking optional.
